## The problem

The move you played is not the move that was there.

You finish a game, you lost by nine points, and somewhere around turn six there
was a bingo sitting on your rack that nobody at the table saw. Scrabble is one of
the few games where the best move is a fact — a finite board, a finite rack, a
fixed word list — and where a human being reliably cannot find it. You can review
a chess game afterwards and be told exactly where you went wrong. There is no
equivalent for the board you just packed away.

Scrubble is that review. You set up the position, you enter the rack, and it
returns every legal move ranked by score. The distinction it is built around is
that it is an analysis board, not an assistant: it is for the game that is over,
the way a chess engine is for the game that is over. A solver you consult mid-turn against
someone sitting opposite you is a different product with the same code in it, and
I did not want to build that one.

## What I chose

Flutter, targeting Android, iOS, macOS, Windows and web from one codebase, with
Riverpod holding board, rack, dictionary and solver state — chosen because its
providers are testable without a widget tree, and most of this project is logic
rather than screens.

The dictionary is a bundled asset — nothing leaves the device and none of it
needs a network — read into a trie at launch. The solver is
anchor-based cross-check move generation, the Appel and Jacobson approach: find
the empty squares adjacent to played tiles, work out for each of them which
letters could legally form a cross-word, build prefixes leftward out of the rack,
then extend rightward across the anchor while the trie says a word can still
continue. Then the same thing again vertically. Scoring applies multipliers only
to newly placed tiles, adds every cross-word the placement creates, and adds fifty
if the rack empties.

## What I rejected

**Permutations of the rack.** The obvious first solver: generate every
arrangement of up to seven tiles, try it at every position, keep what the
dictionary accepts. It is correct, it is about fifteen lines, and it does an
enormous amount of work to discover that no English word starts with "qz". The
trie is what turns that into pruning — the moment no word continues a prefix, the
whole branch is gone — and the anchor pass is what stops it from considering
positions that could not be legal placements in the first place.

**A hash set of words.** A set answers "is this a word" in constant time and
answers nothing else, and move generation is not a membership question. Every
step of it asks whether a prefix can still go somewhere, which is the one question
a set cannot answer and the only question a trie is for. The trie costs more
memory for the same word list and that is the entire trade.

**A GADDAG.** The serious answer, and what a competitive engine uses: storing
every rotation of every word so extension runs both directions from an anchor
without the separate left-part pass. It is roughly five times the memory of a
plain trie, in exchange for speed on a problem where the user is a person waiting
for a list. I would rather spend a second than five times the footprint on every
phone the app runs on.

**Solving on the main thread.** The move generation runs in an isolate. A solve
that janks the UI reads as a broken app rather than a busy one.

## What it cost

Entering a position is the slow part, and the solve is instant. A mid-game board
is thirty-odd taps through a per-cell dialog before you get an answer, which is
tolerable when you are reviewing one interesting position and tedious the moment
you want to review a whole game. The fix is photographing the board, and that is a
different project — reading a fifteen-by-fifteen grid of tiles from a phone camera
at an angle, where one misread cell produces a confident wrong answer.

Adding French was not a translation job. The user interface strings are a file;
the *game* is not. French Scrabble has its own letter distribution, its own point
values and its own treatment of accents, which meant the tile model itself had to
become language-dependent rather than a constant map. That is the kind of thing
you find out in phase ten that you should have known in phase one.

Blank tiles cost more than anything else of their size: each one multiplies the
search by twenty-six and scores as nothing.

## The outcome

It works, on the plan's ten phases, on five platforms, in two languages, with
tests behind each phase. It is also not shipped, and those are different words.
What stands between them is not code: a store listing needs an icon, a privacy
answer, a name that does not borrow a trademark, and a word list whose licence I
can point at — the well-known tournament lists are not the public-domain files
they are assumed to be. None of that is hard. All of it
is unfinished, and saying so is more useful than a screenshot.
